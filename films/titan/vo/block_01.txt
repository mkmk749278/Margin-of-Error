The fifteenth of July, twenty twenty-two. On dive eighty, the Titan submersible's hull monitor recorded a burst of sound, and a sudden shift in strain.

Eleven months later, on the way down to the Titanic, the hull failed. Stockton Rush, Paul-Henri Nargeolet, Shahzada Dawood, Suleman Dawood and Hamish Harding died.

Titan's hull was hard to inspect, so OceanGate relied on this monitor to warn it when the hull was weakening.

It is often said the warning worked, and was ignored. The record is more complicated. The monitor had gaps, its alerts were set on other hulls, and its strain was read against time.

After the implosion, the N - T - S - B, America's accident investigator, plotted the same numbers against depth. We will draw that chart with them, from the reports and the sworn testimony.

The eighteenth of June, twenty twenty-three. At about nine in the morning, Titan was released from its launch platform. It was the first dive of that year's expedition to go deeper than ten metres.

At ten forty-seven, at about three thousand three hundred and fifty metres, Titan sent a routine message: it was dropping two weights, a standard step at that depth to slow the descent. It was the last message Titan sent. A minute later, the support ship acknowledged it, and kept sending messages for hours. No reply came.

Four days later, a remotely operated vehicle found the tail cone and both titanium domes on the sea floor, a few hundred metres from the Titanic's bow.

By its dive log, Titan had reached the wreck, three thousand eight hundred and forty metres down, thirteen times. On its last dive, it failed about five hundred metres shallower.

So where did the margin of error run out? And when did the hull start to go?

At three thousand eight hundred and forty metres, the ocean presses on every surface with about three hundred and eighty times the pressure at the surface.

Titan was not built the way deep submersibles usually are.

Its pressure hull was a cylinder of carbon fibre, five layers, each about an inch thick, bonded one onto the next. Titanium rings were bonded to each end, and titanium domes were bolted to the rings.

According to a former director of marine operations, the company's chief executive chose carbon fibre because he believed it was better in compression than in tension. He also believed that, because it was lighter, it would need less buoyancy, could be launched from many more kinds of ship instead of a purpose-built one, and could carry more people.

How strong was it? The company's original goal was a hull rated to six thousand metres, with a safety factor of two point two five: a hull that should not fail until about thirteen and a half thousand metres.

Canada's Transportation Safety Board found that OceanGate later changed that safety factor to one point two five. The analysis for this hull said the carbon cylinder would hold to about seven thousand five hundred metres: six thousand, times one point two five. Less than twice the depth of the Titanic. And, the N - T - S - B noted, that analysis assumed perfect geometry and perfectly aligned fibres, not the hull that was actually built.

Scale models built with the new materials imploded, or nearly imploded, shallower than three thousand metres. OceanGate traced those failures to wrinkles in the carbon, and changed its method to prevent them.

That new method, bonding the layers one onto the next, was never tested on a model. Canada's investigators found that a scale model built that way was discussed, and was not built, in order to save money. The only one ever built was the hull that carried people.

In early twenty twenty-one, that hull went to a pressure test facility in Maryland. The test plan had it going to the pressure of four thousand two hundred metres on three of the four days.

The facility's logbook shows it reached that pressure once. The first of March, for twenty minutes.

According to analysis done for the company, the test depth was limited by a titanium dome, not by the carbon. On the other days, the hull was held near four thousand metres, and then at three thousand eight hundred and forty, for four hours, twice.

It was never tested to failure. The N - T - S - B found it unlikely that the hull ever had the strength the analysis predicted, and that the company did not know its actual strength.

Then how would anyone know if it was failing?

OceanGate's answer was to listen to it. Eight acoustic sensors and sixteen strain gauges, fixed inside the hull. A carbon hull, the theory went, would crackle as it weakened, and the company would retire it in time.

The system had limits. The pilot saw live readings, but the full data were downloaded only after each dive. Acoustic data were analysed only from the dive itself, so damage at the surface, or in storage, could not raise an alert.

In twenty twenty-two, three of the eight acoustic channels registered no acoustic events all season.

After each dive, the engineering director looked at the data first, and emailed it to about eight to ten people, the chief executive among them.

And the alert criteria had been set on the sounds of earlier hulls and scale models, built a different way. No model had ever been built this hull's way, so, the N - T - S - B found, OceanGate had no data on how this one would sound as it failed.

Had anyone warned about this?

The eighteenth of January, twenty eighteen. The company's director of marine operations filed an inspection report on Titan's first hull. It listed twenty-six items needing action.

On the hull, he wrote that the cut-off ends of the cylinder showed very visible signs of delamination, and he warned of potential inter-laminar fatigue due to pressure cycling.

The next day, he met the chief executive and argued for more testing of the hull. The chief executive recorded the meeting. On that recording, he answered that the cut-off ends were scrap, cut away for that reason, and that acoustic monitoring would tell him way before the hull failed.

Four days later, the director was fired. The termination letter said the two of them were at an impasse over the hull.

In March, more than twenty submersible professionals signed a letter urging the chief executive to put safety first, and to follow the industry's established practices. It was never sent formally. He received it, called the head of the industry committee behind it, and said he was leaving the association.

That was the first hull. But the failure mode named in that report, delamination, is what the investigators would find in the second.

In twenty nineteen, a crack was found in the first hull. The company's first director of engineering testified that he refused to sign it off, and that his refusal kept it from that year's expedition. He said it angered the chief executive, and led to his firing. The company's letter to him cited the cancelled expedition.

A second hull was built: the one lost in twenty twenty-three. Its dives were numbered fifty to eighty-eight.

Now, dive eighty.

On the fifteenth of July, twenty twenty-two, Titan reached the Titanic and came back up. Near the surface, the people inside heard a loud bang. So did the crew of a small boat above. The records differ on exactly where it happened, but all place it near the surface.

On channel two, the acoustic sensor peaked above twenty-five thousand millivolts. At the same moment, the strain gauges beside it jumped.

Phil Brooks was the director of engineering by then, promoted from software engineer before that season. He testified that he had no background in mechanical engineering or materials, and that the agreement was that others, mainly the chief executive, would handle those tasks.

Under oath, he described what they did after the bang. They inspected the hull as best they could. But the inside was lined by an insert, and the outside was covered. You can take a flashlight, he said, and look down a small gap between the insert and the hull. In his words: it's not a great test.

The insert, he said, was never removed. That was never done.

So the main check was the data. The strain showed a shift in level, on two of the sixteen channels. A sudden shift, he said, is something to be concerned about, for sure. He added that he was not a strain expert, and that this one looked fairly minor.

He was asked who made the decision to resume diving.

Definitely Stockton, he said.

And he described the plan, in his words: I think the plan was at that time to, well, let's dive again and see what happens, on eighty-one, on the next dives.

What did they think the bang was? Brooks testified that the chief executive's theory was the frame. The hull compresses slightly on the way down and the frame bolted to it does not, so on the way up the frame may have sprung, and snapped back into shape. Brooks said that, without being a qualified mechanical engineer, he found it plausible at the time.

A contractor on that expedition testified that she asked him whether the data could show delamination. In her account, he said it was only a few microns, and that the next two dives would go ahead, with a visual inspection back in Seattle. Brooks himself testified that they had converted the shift into a physical distance, and judged it a relatively small movement.
