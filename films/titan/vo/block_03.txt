Two things often said about Titan. Its controller: on dive eighty-one, the thruster controls were found to be mapped in reverse, and the pilot turned the controller around. None of the three reports names the controller as a cause. Its window: the Coast Guard board criticised it as untested and uncertified, but found it unlikely that the window caused the implosion. All three reports place the failure in the hull.

The N - T - S - B's probable cause was OceanGate's inadequate engineering process, which failed to establish the actual strength and durability of the Titan pressure vessel.

Contributing were the guidance and the rules that did not make OceanGate meet the industry's standards, and the company's flawed analysis of its own monitoring data.

The Coast Guard's Marine Board called it a preventable tragedy.

Canada's Transportation Safety Board found that Titan received no oversight from Transport Canada, though it sailed from Saint John's with Canadian ships.

The Coast Guard board also wrote this, about Mr. Rush, who was both the company's chief executive and its pilot. Had he survived the incident, the board would have recommended that the Commandant refer the matter to the Department of Justice, for its consideration on whether to pursue a separate criminal investigation.

That investigation, the board noted, will not occur, because Mr. Rush died.

One more finding concerns the eighteenth of June itself. The board found that the company's own communications plan delayed the first call to rescuers by more than seven hours, and that no deep-water rescue vehicle was standing by. It also found that the implosion caused the immediate loss of all lives on board, before any distress call. The N - T - S - B found that search and rescue could not have saved them. With assets ready, it found, Titan would likely have been found sooner.

OceanGate suspended its operations in July twenty twenty-three. When the Coast Guard report came out, a company spokesperson said OceanGate had been wound down, was fully cooperating with the investigation, and offered condolences to the families.

What now? The N - T - S - B made four recommendations to the U - S Coast Guard. One: propose that the International Maritime Organization make its guidance for submersibles mandatory. Two: study crewed pressure vessels. Three: write rules for them. Four: bring its nineteen ninety-three guidance up to date with the law passed that same year. The Coast Guard agreed to update its guidance, and partly agreed with the rest. As of October twenty twenty-six, all four recommendations remain open.

On the fifteenth of July, twenty twenty-two, Titan's hull changed, and its own sensors recorded it.

Eleven months later, on its first deep dive since, five people were aboard.
