## Fairness (US and UK defamation norms)

- **Order of the first 30 seconds.** Bang, jump, "Rush wanted to keep diving", two more dives, five dead: the run
  implies a man kept diving a hull he knew was damaged. Sentences 6–9 then say the record is "more complicated" and
  that the company "did not know its hull was damaged" (NTSB). The opening therefore corrects its own implication
  within about 25 seconds, and the correction is the record's finding. Keep sentences 6–9 together with 1–5: if the
  opening is ever cut short (a Short, a trailer), sentences 3–5 must not run alone.
- **Rush** (deceased; no defamation claim lies for the dead in the US or England and Wales, but the channel's fairness
  rule still applies): every statement about him is either sworn testimony attributed to the witness (sentence 3,
  the clip) or an investigator's reading (NTSB p. 36 gives the same words as the CEO's). No motive, no knowledge, no
  criminal language. Keep it that way: no "knew", "ignored", "gambled" in our voice.
- **Brooks** (living, named on the lower third): described only in his public role, with an accurate tenure; his own
  hedge ("I think the plan was") is heard, not removed; nothing implies he made the decision (he named Rush, C33).
- **OceanGate** (company, wind-down): "did not know" is the NTSB's finding and exculpatory as to knowledge; "a company
  that read this data" must not overstate how often it did (sentence 9 FIX).
- **Others:** the mission specialists and the boat crew are not named; the contractor dispute (USCG fn 46: refuted by
  the Director of Administration) is not in the opening. No pending case is referred to; if one is added later, it is
  said as pending.
- **"It is often said"** is a fair, sourced description of public opinion, not an allegation by us, and the next
  sentence qualifies it.
