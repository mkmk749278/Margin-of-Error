The fifteenth of July, twenty twenty-two. On dive eighty, the Titan submersible's hull monitor recorded a burst of sound, and a sudden shift in strain.

Eleven months later, on the way down to the Titanic, the hull failed. Stockton Rush, Paul-Henri Nargeolet, Shahzada Dawood, Suleman Dawood and Hamish Harding died.

Titan's hull was hard to inspect, so OceanGate relied on this monitor to warn it when the hull was weakening.

It is often said the warning worked, and was ignored. The record is more complicated. The monitor had gaps, its alerts were set on other hulls, and its strain was read against time.

After the implosion, the N - T - S - B, America's accident investigator, plotted the same numbers against depth. We will draw that chart with them, from the reports and the sworn testimony.

The eighteenth of June, twenty twenty-three. At about nine in the morning, Titan was released from its launch platform. It was the first dive of that year's expedition to go deeper than ten metres.

At ten forty-seven, at about three thousand three hundred and fifty metres, Titan sent a routine message: it was dropping two weights, a standard step at that depth to slow the descent. It was the last message Titan sent. A minute later, the support ship acknowledged it, and kept sending messages for hours. No reply came.

Four days later, a remotely operated vehicle found the tail cone and both titanium domes on the sea floor, a few hundred metres from the Titanic's bow.

By its dive log, Titan had reached the wreck, three thousand eight hundred and forty metres down, thirteen times. On its last dive, it failed about five hundred metres shallower.

So where did the margin of error run out? And when did the hull start to go?

At three thousand eight hundred and forty metres, the ocean presses on every surface with about three hundred and eighty times the pressure at the surface.

Titan was not built the way deep submersibles usually are.

Its pressure hull was a cylinder of carbon fibre, five layers, each about an inch thick, bonded one onto the next. Titanium rings were bonded to each end, and titanium domes were bolted to the rings.

According to a former director of marine operations, the company's chief executive chose carbon fibre because he believed it was better in compression than in tension. He also believed that, because it was lighter, it would need less buoyancy, could be launched from many more kinds of ship instead of a purpose-built one, and could carry more people.

How strong was it? The company's original goal was a hull rated to six thousand metres, with a safety factor of two point two five: a hull that should not fail until about thirteen and a half thousand metres.

Canada's Transportation Safety Board found that OceanGate later changed that safety factor to one point two five. The analysis for this hull said the carbon cylinder would hold to about seven thousand five hundred metres: six thousand, times one point two five. Less than twice the depth of the Titanic. And, the N - T - S - B noted, that analysis assumed perfect geometry and perfectly aligned fibres, not the hull that was actually built.

Scale models built with the new materials imploded, or nearly imploded, shallower than three thousand metres. OceanGate traced those failures to wrinkles in the carbon, and changed its method to prevent them.

That new method, bonding the layers one onto the next, was never tested on a model. Canada's investigators found that a scale model built that way was discussed, and was not built, in order to save money. The only one ever built was the hull that carried people.

In early twenty twenty-one, that hull went to a pressure test facility in Maryland. The test plan had it going to the pressure of four thousand two hundred metres on three of the four days.

The facility's logbook shows it reached that pressure once. The first of March, for twenty minutes.

According to analysis done for the company, the test depth was limited by a titanium dome, not by the carbon. On the other days, the hull was held near four thousand metres, and then at three thousand eight hundred and forty, for four hours, twice.

It was never tested to failure. The N - T - S - B found it unlikely that the hull ever had the strength the analysis predicted, and that the company did not know its actual strength.

Then how would anyone know if it was failing?

OceanGate's answer was to listen to it. Eight acoustic sensors and sixteen strain gauges, fixed inside the hull. A carbon hull, the theory went, would crackle as it weakened, and the company would retire it in time.

The system had limits. The pilot saw live readings, but the full data were downloaded only after each dive. Acoustic data were analysed only from the dive itself, so damage at the surface, or in storage, could not raise an alert.

In twenty twenty-two, three of the eight acoustic channels registered no acoustic events all season.

After each dive, the engineering director looked at the data first, and emailed it to about eight to ten people, the chief executive among them.

And the alert criteria had been set on the sounds of earlier hulls and scale models, built a different way. No model had ever been built this hull's way, so, the N - T - S - B found, OceanGate had no data on how this one would sound as it failed.

Had anyone warned about this?

The eighteenth of January, twenty eighteen. The company's director of marine operations filed an inspection report on Titan's first hull. It listed twenty-six items needing action.

On the hull, he wrote that the cut-off ends of the cylinder showed very visible signs of delamination, and he warned of potential inter-laminar fatigue due to pressure cycling.

The next day, he met the chief executive and argued for more testing of the hull. The chief executive recorded the meeting. On that recording, he answered that the cut-off ends were scrap, cut away for that reason, and that acoustic monitoring would tell him way before the hull failed.

Four days later, the director was fired. The termination letter said the two of them were at an impasse over the hull.

In March, more than twenty submersible professionals signed a letter urging the chief executive to put safety first, and to follow the industry's established practices. It was never sent formally. He received it, called the head of the industry committee behind it, and said he was leaving the association.

That was the first hull. But the failure mode named in that report, delamination, is what the investigators would find in the second.

In twenty nineteen, a crack was found in the first hull. The company's first director of engineering testified that he refused to sign it off, and that his refusal kept it from that year's expedition. He said it angered the chief executive, and led to his firing. The company's letter to him cited the cancelled expedition.

A second hull was built: the one lost in twenty twenty-three. Its dives were numbered fifty to eighty-eight.

Now, dive eighty.

On the fifteenth of July, twenty twenty-two, Titan reached the Titanic and came back up. Near the surface, the people inside heard a loud bang. So did the crew of a small boat above. The records differ on exactly where it happened, but all place it near the surface.

On channel two, the acoustic sensor peaked above twenty-five thousand millivolts. At the same moment, the strain gauges beside it jumped.

Phil Brooks was the director of engineering by then, promoted from software engineer before that season. He testified that he had no background in mechanical engineering or materials, and that the agreement was that others, mainly the chief executive, would handle those tasks.

Under oath, he described what they did after the bang. They inspected the hull as best they could. But the inside was lined by an insert, and the outside was covered. You can take a flashlight, he said, and look down a small gap between the insert and the hull. In his words: it's not a great test.

The insert, he said, was never removed. That was never done.

So the main check was the data. The strain showed a shift in level, on two of the sixteen channels. A sudden shift, he said, is something to be concerned about, for sure. He added that he was not a strain expert, and that this one looked fairly minor.

He was asked who made the decision to resume diving.

Definitely Stockton, he said.

And he described the plan, in his words: I think the plan was at that time to, well, let's dive again and see what happens, on eighty-one, on the next dives.

What did they think the bang was? Brooks testified that the chief executive's theory was the frame. The hull compresses slightly on the way down and the frame bolted to it does not, so on the way up the frame may have sprung, and snapped back into shape. Brooks said that, without being a qualified mechanical engineer, he found it plausible at the time.

A contractor on that expedition testified that she asked him whether the data could show delamination. In her account, he said it was only a few microns, and that the next two dives would go ahead, with a visual inspection back in Seattle. Brooks himself testified that they had converted the shift into a physical distance, and judged it a relatively small movement.

Titan dived again on the nineteenth of July. Brooks testified that after that dive, they didn't see any further shifts in strain data. No loud bangs. Nothing in the acoustics that seemed out of the ordinary.

One acoustic sensor, he testified, did show activity on the dives that followed that they had not seen in twenty twenty-one, the kind of thing they wanted to be concerned about. He raised it with the chief executive. It stayed under the threshold they had set, he said, or maybe just barely over it, and it never got worse, so he did not think it was a concern. The N - T - S - B found the sensors never produced the kind of signal that would have triggered an alert.

So, was the damage in the data?

Brooks testified that what he looked for in the strain was linearity with depth. The depth was there, but on its own chart beside the strain, and both ran against the clock. Every dive has its own shape, its own pace, its own stops. Laid side by side, the dives are hard to compare.

The N - T - S - B said a more appropriate way was to plot strain against depth directly, because depth sets the pressure.

So here is the same data, against depth.

Dive seventy-five, a straight line. Dive eighty, the same straight line, until the event at its very end.

Dive eighty-one. Dive eighty-two. Dive eighty-three. In the first five hundred metres, every one of them bends away.

To put a number on it, the N - T - S - B fitted a line to the shallowest stretch, from fifteen to a hundred and fifty metres: metres of depth for each millivolt from the gauge. Before the event: five point zero zero, and four point seven four. After it: eight point seven six, eight point zero nine, eight point seven seven.

Same gauge, same hull, same sea. A response that had changed by about three quarters.

Could OceanGate have seen this at the time? The Coast Guard board found that OceanGate had all the data it needed to show the hull's behaviour had changed, and that analysing strain against elapsed time failed to reveal it. The timing that follows is our own reading of the record, not a finding of any report.

Brooks was right that there was no new jump. What changed was the slope, and against time a change in slope is hard to see.

Dive eighty itself shows nothing until its last minutes. In the N - T - S - B's plots, the bend first appears in dive eighty-one. That data was on the company's computer after that dive. Plotted against depth, it would have shown the bend before dive eighty-two, three days later, and before dive eighty-three.

And it was not a faulty gauge. Deeper down, the curves still match, which the N - T - S - B said shows the gauges had not changed. The hull had.

Canada's investigators later measured the strain by depth their own way: each dive against itself, not dive against dive. On the nineteenth of July, the slope from ten to three hundred metres was nearly double the slope for the whole dive. They also found a smaller bend a year earlier, in August twenty twenty-one, and could not determine whether the bang caused the change.

Why would a damaged hull bend only near the surface?

The N - T - S - B found long voids in the bond between the first and second layers, and between the third and fourth. Where a layer separates at the end of the cylinder, it can move on its own under light pressure. Deeper down, it found, the layers respond like the intact hull.

Titan made three more dives that July. Two of them, eighty-one and eighty-two, still reached the Titanic, which the N - T - S - B said showed the first damage was not enough to make it implode. A delamination is not a crack through the wall. But the wall was no longer what it had been.

The N - T - S - B's finding: because its analysis was flawed, OceanGate was unable to identify that the Titan was damaged after dive eighty, and that it needed to be immediately removed from service.

Then it had to spend the winter somewhere.

On the twenty-sixth of July, Titan was set down in a parking lot at a marine base in Saint John's, Newfoundland. Uncovered.

Two days later, the base passed on a quote for a protective cover: one thousand seven hundred and fifty Canadian dollars. The base told the Coast Guard board that OceanGate never answered that email.

In September, the base wrote again: Titan still had not been covered. OceanGate's logistics director said it would be discussed. The board found no action followed.

It stayed outside until the sixth of February, through temperatures from one point four to eighty-four point two degrees Fahrenheit, about minus seventeen to twenty-nine Celsius, through freezing rain, sleet and snow.

Brooks testified that what they really wanted was to bring Titan back to Everett, pull the insert, and look inside the hull for cracks. They were told the cost of shipping it back was prohibitive.

Was money behind these decisions? The Coast Guard board says it was: in its words, a troubling prioritization of cost and time-saving measures over safety. The N - T - S - B's findings do not address money.

From June twenty twenty-two to May twenty twenty-three, the board found, the chief executive advanced the company one point eight five million dollars. The board found that employees were asked to temporarily forgo their salaries, with a promise of back pay.

The ship that had carried Titan was unavailable for twenty twenty-three, the board found. Titan would be towed to the site on its launch platform, behind a smaller ship.

Early in twenty twenty-three, Brooks left. Under oath, he said his main reason was that plan to tow Titan, which meant working on its platform in open seas, and that the reasons for it were economic. In his words: I felt that the safety was just being compromised way too much. At least for myself. He said he suggested they not go, and was told that wasn't possible: they had people that paid.

The board found that the chief executive then left the director of engineering post vacant, in its words, to ensure the twenty twenty-three expedition proceeded unhindered.

And the board drew the line back to twenty nineteen, when, by his own testimony, the first director of engineering had kept a damaged hull from an expedition. Leaving the post empty, it wrote, removed the possibility of a replacement director blocking the expedition.

That spring, at sea and in port, the platform tipped more than once. Once, in May, its bow went partly under water, and Titan's tail cone was lost.

How was a hull like this allowed to carry passengers?

By the N - T - S - B's reading of the law, it was not. Under U - S law, the N - T - S - B notes, a submersible carrying even one passenger for hire is a small passenger vessel, and must be inspected by the Coast Guard. OceanGate never registered Titan, and never arranged an inspection.

The Coast Guard board found that OceanGate described its paying passengers as mission specialists, with mandatory duties, to conceal that they were paying passengers.

The regulator's own guidance gets the same scrutiny. The Coast Guard's voluntary guidance for passenger submersibles was published in July nineteen ninety-three. That December, a new law changed the definition, to cover any submersible with one passenger for hire.

As of its report in October twenty twenty-five, the N - T - S - B found, the guidance had never been updated to match. The Coast Guard, it found, had no specific inspection standards for how a submersible is designed or built.

That guidance also recommended that a submersible dive no deeper than its rescue could reach. The N - T - S - B found that Titan regularly dived to the Titanic with no rescue standing by that could reach it.

The N - T - S - B found that voluntary guidance and the rules for small passenger vessels were insufficient to make OceanGate follow the industry's established standards.

In twenty twenty-three, Titan's first four dives went no deeper than ten metres.

At some point after dive eighty-two, the hull was damaged again. The N - T - S - B calls that damage of unknown origin. The Coast Guard board says the damage from dive eighty was likely made worse by the winter outdoors, and points to the towing as well.

Did every dive weaken it? Here the investigators differ. The N - T - S - B dates two events: dive eighty, and something after dive eighty-two. Canada's Transportation Safety Board found the cylinder likely failed progressively, with damage accumulating during each dive.

On the eighteenth of June, Titan made its first deep dive in eleven months.

The N - T - S - B found that the existing delaminations and the later damage likely led to a local buckling failure. A delamination splits one thick layer into two thinner ones, and thinner layers buckle more easily. In a local buckle, one part of the wall changes shape, and gives way. The damage from dive eighty, it found, had reached the forward end of the cylinder: that is where the gauge that showed the change sat.

Two things often said about Titan. Its controller: on dive eighty-one, the thruster controls were found to be mapped in reverse, and the pilot turned the controller around. None of the three reports names the controller as a cause. Its window: the Coast Guard board criticised it as untested and uncertified, but found it unlikely that the window caused the implosion. All three reports place the failure in the hull.

The N - T - S - B's probable cause was OceanGate's inadequate engineering process, which failed to establish the actual strength and durability of the Titan pressure vessel.

Contributing were the guidance and the rules that did not make OceanGate meet the industry's standards, and the company's flawed analysis of its own monitoring data.

The Coast Guard's Marine Board called it a preventable tragedy.

Canada's Transportation Safety Board found that Titan received no oversight from Transport Canada, though it sailed from Saint John's with Canadian ships.

The Coast Guard board also wrote this, about Mr. Rush, who was both the company's chief executive and its pilot. Had he survived the incident, the board would have recommended that the Commandant refer the matter to the Department of Justice, for its consideration on whether to pursue a separate criminal investigation.

Whether any crime was committed, the board wrote, was for the Justice Department to decide, and that will not happen, because Mr. Rush died.

One more finding concerns the eighteenth of June itself. The board found that the company's own communications plan delayed the first call to rescuers by more than seven hours, and that no deep-water rescue vehicle was standing by. It also found that the implosion caused the immediate loss of all lives on board, before any distress call. The N - T - S - B found that search and rescue could not have saved them. With assets ready, it found, Titan would likely have been found sooner.

OceanGate suspended its operations in July twenty twenty-three. When the Coast Guard report came out, a company spokesperson said OceanGate had been wound down, was fully cooperating with the investigation, and offered condolences to the families.

What now? The N - T - S - B made four recommendations to the U - S Coast Guard. One: propose that the International Maritime Organization make its guidance for submersibles mandatory. Two: study pressure vessels that carry people. Three: write rules for them. Four: bring its nineteen ninety-three guidance up to date with the law passed that same year. The Coast Guard agreed to update its guidance, and partly agreed with the rest. As of October twenty twenty-six, all four recommendations remain open.

On the fifteenth of July, twenty twenty-two, Titan's hull changed, and its own sensors recorded it.

Eleven months later, on its first deep dive since, five people were aboard.

Stockton Rush. Paul-Henri Nargeolet. Shahzada Dawood. Suleman Dawood. Hamish Harding.
