The third of February, twenty twenty-three. Eight thirteen in the evening. Salem, Ohio.

A freight train is passing a factory, and a surveillance camera records a fire under one of its cars. The twenty-third car, a covered hopper. The fire is at a wheel, at the bearing on its leading axle.

Moments later the train crosses a hot bearing detector, an infrared sensor beside the track. It reads that bearing at one hundred and three degrees Fahrenheit above the air temperature.

The line at which the crew would be told to slow down and stop is one hundred and seventy.

So the detector sends an alert. Not to the train, but to a help desk. In the cab, nothing.

The train is Norfolk Southern thirty-two N. One hundred and forty-nine cars, nine thousand, three hundred and nine feet long.

Why that line sat at one hundred and seventy is on the record, in the railroad industry's own words, at a federal hearing. We will hear them later in this film.

Thirty-nine minutes after Salem, the train crosses the next detector, in East Palestine. This time the reading is two hundred and fifty-three degrees above the air. That is the highest number this kind of detector can show. The real temperature may have been higher.

In the cab, the speakers announce it. Critical alarm, critical alarm, critical alarm.

The engineer begins to slow the train. The N - T - S - B, America's accident investigator, would find that the crew handled the train properly and responded to the alarm as they should. Less than a minute after the alarm, the axle came apart.

Thirty-eight cars left the track, and the wreckage caught fire. About two thousand people were told to leave their homes. This film follows the warning system and the decisions that followed. The health and environmental effects are a separate story.

Three days later, a second number would decide what happened to five tank cars of vinyl chloride. The investigators could not find it in the written guidance used at the scene.

Two numbers: one on the first night, one on the fourth day. So where did the margin of error run out?

Each end of each axle on a railcar turns inside a roller bearing, sealed and packed with grease. When a bearing starts to fail, friction turns into heat.

So railroads line their tracks with detectors that look up at each bearing as it passes, and measure how much hotter it is than the air.
```

```
On Norfolk Southern, three lines decided what happened next. Ninety degrees above the air: an alert to the help desk, which watches the next readings. One hundred and seventy: a message to the crew, who must slow down, and then stop to inspect. And a critical alarm, for which the crew must stop at once. Before this accident, Norfolk Southern set that critical line at two hundred degrees.

At Salem the reading was one hundred and three. Above the first line. Below the second.

But the camera showed fire. How could the bearing read so low? The detector is a pair of infrared sensors looking up from the track, and at Salem they read the inner edge of the bearing's outer cup. A failing bearing heats from the inside, and the outside lags behind. The N - T - S - B found that the Salem reading did not reflect the bearing's true temperature. And once a bearing is failing, the report notes, it can go from below an alarm temperature to seized in less than a minute.

The analyst on the help desk did not see the Salem alert. He was working on other, higher-priority alerts. The investigators found it would not have changed the outcome: the train derailed before the procedures required him to act.

If you have read about this derailment, you may have heard a simple answer: a skipped repair, a bearing with no grease. Hold that thought. The record can't confirm it. What it does show is stranger: where the warning line came from.

The detectors themselves were miles apart. From Salem to East Palestine, about nineteen miles. Norfolk Southern testified that its average spacing on key routes was thirteen point nine. The spacing met the industry's recommended practice, forty miles or less. At the detector before Salem, in Sebring, about eleven miles back, the same bearing read thirty-eight degrees, below the alert line. From there to a broken axle: about thirty miles. Both can be true at once: a slow build over many miles, then a failure in under a minute.

At Salem the reading was over the help desk line and under the crew line. Had the crew line been lower, the crew would have been told. Whether that would have stopped this train in time, the N - T - S - B did not decide: it found that research is needed to tell whether lower alert and alarm thresholds would make a significant safety improvement. Either way: where did one hundred and seventy come from?
```

```
The answer is in the industry's rulebook. The A - A - R, the Association of American Railroads, defines an overheated bearing as one at least one hundred and seventy degrees above the air. The N - T - S - B found that this definition is based on what makes a repair billable to the railcar's owner. It relates, the board wrote, to billing for repairs, not directly to safety.

In June twenty twenty-three, at a public hearing in East Palestine, the board's technical panel put that to the A - A - R's senior vice president for safety and operations.

Then a member of the N - T - S - B's technical panel asked whether these thresholds are limits for safe operation at all.

A billing standard. And, in the same breath, the industry's point: no railroad wants a derailment. The witness went on to explain that the association can set standards only for cars passed between railroads, not for what a railroad does on its own track.

Norfolk Southern testified that it based its own thresholds on its experience and on the industry's standards. Its critical alarm, before the accident, was two hundred. After it, one hundred and seventy. At the same hearing, the industry's witness said the railroads were now all committed to using one hundred and seventy. So after East Palestine, on this railroad, the number in the billing definition became the line at which a train must stop at once.

Every line like this is a trade. Set it too low, and trains stop for nothing. Set it too high, and a failing bearing keeps rolling. In the N - T - S - B's words, railroads choose their thresholds by balancing between preventing bearing failures and avoiding unnecessary stops.

No investigator links what follows to this bearing. But it is how the railroad was being run. In February twenty nineteen, Norfolk Southern told investors its plan. Precision scheduled railroading. An operating ratio, the share of revenue spent running the railroad, of sixty percent by twenty twenty-one, down from sixty-five point four. Continued share buybacks, using free cash flow and borrowing capacity. Its five principles included working safely.

The filings show what followed. Average employees fell from twenty-six thousand, six hundred and sixty-two in twenty eighteen, the year before the plan, to eighteen thousand, nine hundred in twenty twenty-two: by our calculation, twenty-nine percent. From twenty nineteen to twenty twenty-two, the company spent ten billion dollars buying back its own shares.
```

```
Did that cause this derailment? Here is where the record stops.

The twenty-third car was not Norfolk Southern's. It belonged to G - A - T - X, and was leased to Braskem America. It joined the train at a yard in Illinois run by a terminal railroad, part-owned by several large railroads, Norfolk Southern among them. That railroad's inspectors checked it. Norfolk Southern's inspectors did not. Whether that inspection missed anything, the investigators could not determine.

After the derailment, the N - T - S - B checked the cars that stayed on the rails, and found reportable defects on about a quarter of them, most of them minor. It obtained a Norfolk Southern foreman's email that appeared to set a ninety-second target for each car's inspection in Norfolk Southern's own yards. Car twenty-three was inspected elsewhere. And it could not determine whether inspection time goals contributed to the defects that went unreported.

In March twenty twenty-three, the N - T - S - B opened a special investigation into Norfolk Southern's safety culture.

That is our reading of the record: no investigator links the plan to this bearing. What the record does show is where the stop line was drawn: at a number the industry wrote for billing, which Norfolk Southern says it combined with its own experience.

A claim you will find in many comment sections: cost cutting caused this. The record can't say.

Back to the bearing. As it fails, the rollers and the cup grind and heat. The grease burns. The seals go. Then the end of the axle, the journal, breaks away, and the wheelset drops.

Watch the last nineteen miles.

From Salem, the train runs east, at between nineteen and forty-five miles an hour. Between the detectors, nothing measures the bearing. Nineteen miles of track, and nothing reads it.

Milepost forty-nine point eight one. Two hundred and fifty-three degrees, the top of the scale. Critical alarm. The crew begins to slow the train. Less than a minute later, the axle separates, and the brakes go into emergency on their own. From the emergency, the lead locomotives travel another one thousand, one hundred and sixty feet, and stop thirty-eight seconds later.
```

```
So was it the grease? The record can't say. The investigators found that the root cause of the bearing's failure remains unclear. Whatever started it, fatigue, a loss of lubrication, water, was erased by the heat of its own failure. And the N - T - S - B noted that this is a pattern: a bearing that fails badly enough to derail a train tends to destroy the evidence of why.

Another claim: the burn, three days later, destroyed that evidence. That is not in the record. Investigators recovered the bearing's pieces on the fifth of February, west of the wreck, where they had fallen in the first moments. The burn was on the sixth, on five other cars.

The fire, the N - T - S - B found, likely began with a tank car. In the derailment, a D - O - T one eleven tank car carrying a flammable liquid was punctured, and its contents fed the fire. In the same pile lay five pressure tank cars of vinyl chloride. Heat from the fire set off the relief valves on four of them, the valves that open when the pressure inside a tank gets too high.

The responders did not yet know what was on the train. The N - T - S - B found that Norfolk Southern's delay in sending the list of cars kept responders near the fire longer than they needed to be, and delayed the evacuation order.

Vinyl chloride is the raw material of P - V - C, a plastic. Its molecules can join into long chains, and that reaction gives off heat. In a closed tank, a runaway reaction could build pressure until the tank fails. That was the fear.

Now the second number.

On the evening of the fourth of February, the shipper of the vinyl chloride, Oxy Vinyls, told Norfolk Southern and its contractors it saw a low probability of that reaction.

The hottest car's temperature, measured by those contractors, peaked at about one hundred and thirty-eight degrees Fahrenheit. This time that is the actual temperature, not a number above the air. By the afternoon of the sixth, it had fallen to about one hundred and twenty-six.

That afternoon the incident commander, the village's fire chief, met the governors of Ohio and Pennsylvania and dozens of other officials. Oxy Vinyls was not invited, the incident commander told investigators. The N - T - S - B found no evidence that anyone outside Norfolk Southern and its contractors knew the shipper disagreed.

Then came a smaller meeting. Two contractor firms told him that if a car reached one hundred and fifty-three to one hundred and fifty-eight degrees, the reaction would run out of control. The investigators did not find that range in the written guidance consulted at the scene, or in the shipper's messages. The incident commander said he was given thirteen minutes to decide. The burn, they told him, had to start before three in the afternoon, to finish in daylight and avoid a temperature inversion, a layer of air that would keep the vapor cloud from dispersing.

The incident commander consented. The charges went off at four thirty-seven. Three in the afternoon, the N - T - S - B noted, was not a hard deadline.

Two numbers decided this town's week. One matched a definition written for billing. The other, investigators could not find in the guidance used at the scene.

The N - T - S - B's finding: the temperatures on scene did not show that reaction, and examinations afterwards confirmed it. The vent and burn was not necessary. Norfolk Southern and its contractors, the board found, compromised the integrity of the decision by creating unwarranted urgency. The board also found that one industry guide consulted at the scene overstated the risk of that reaction.

Norfolk Southern's answer: the only motivation of the company and its contractors was the health and safety of the community and the first responders, and the incident command had been told about twenty hours ahead that a burn might be needed. The company does not agree that it withheld the shipper's views. The incident commander told investigators that he bore the responsibility.

One more claim you will often see: they burned it because it was the cheapest option. That is not in the record. What is in the record is the urgency, the information the incident commander was given, and the reason Norfolk Southern gives. The N - T - S - B itself could not determine the main reason for the company's preference for the burn.

In June twenty twenty-four, the N - T - S - B adopted its final report. The probable cause: the failure of the bearing on the twenty-third car, which overheated and caused the axle to separate.

Norfolk Southern's annual reports put its net cost from the derailment, after insurance, at about one point one billion dollars in twenty twenty-three, and three hundred and twenty-five million in twenty twenty-four. In twenty twenty-five, insurance and other recoveries were larger than new costs, by two hundred and fifty-four million.

A class action settlement created a six hundred million dollar fund for residents and businesses. The appeals against the settlement were dismissed. An appeal over residents' personal injury releases was still pending in October twenty twenty-six. A separate settlement with the federal government still awaited the court's approval at that date. In March twenty twenty-six, letting residents join that case, the judge's order repeated words from an earlier hearing: this is not a rubber stamp court.

Could it happen again? As of July twenty twenty-six, federal rules did not require trackside detectors at all, or set the temperature at which a car must be stopped. The N - T - S - B has recommended that the F - R - A, the federal railroad regulator, set minimum requirements, including alarm thresholds and the distance between detectors.

Norfolk Southern said in twenty twenty-four that it was installing one hundred and eighty-seven more detectors, to bring the average spacing on its core network to about twelve miles.

And the kind of tank car the investigators found likely started the fire stays legal in flammable service until the first of May, twenty twenty-nine. Retiring them sooner, the investigators found, is feasible, but unlikely, because of economic and business disincentives.

Two last claims. The brake rule repealed in twenty eighteen: it applied to a different kind of train, and this train did not qualify. Too few crew: there were three people in the cab.

As of July twenty twenty-six, no federal rule sets the temperature at which a train must stop. The N - T - S - B has asked for one.

From below the alert line to a broken axle: about thirty miles. The last two detectors were nineteen miles apart.
