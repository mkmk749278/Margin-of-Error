# Treatment review v1

*Independent review of the East Palestine treatment. I judged the plan only as written, with no outside research, and took the claim IDs as accurate.*

---

## 1. Click and retention

### Title/thumbnail pairs

| | Title | Thumbnail | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | "East Palestine: The Wheel Was on Fire. The Detector Read 103°." | 103° amber, ghosted 170° line, burning wheel | **Best click for this audience.** The gap between "on fire" and "103°" is a real puzzle, and engineers and pilots will click to find out why those two facts fit together. Two problems. First, **103° is "above ambient"**. The logline says so, but the title, thumbnail and cold-open voiceover ("The detector read one hundred and three degrees") drop it. The first engineer in the comments will call it a factual error, and this audience punishes that hard. Use "103° above ambient" in the voiceover and "+103°F" in the graphic. Second, a curious general viewer can't read "103° / ALARM 170°" on a phone. Make the 170 line bright and labelled "STOP LINE 170°", and make the 103 bar visibly short of it. |
| **B** | "Why Norfolk Southern's Alarm Came 19 Miles Too Late" | Route line, three readings | **Don't use.** It is the only title that names the company as the subject of a charge, and it is inaccurate. The alarm at East Palestine wasn't late. The problem was the gap between detectors and where the line sat. It will draw the More Perfect Union audience and disappoint them, and it gives NS a fair complaint. |
| **C** | "The Billing Rule Behind the East Palestine Alarm" | Transcript page, "CORRECT." | **Strongest unique hook, weakest thumbnail.** "Billing rule" is the phrase no other film has, and the distrust-PR viewer wants exactly that. But "CORRECT." on a page means nothing before you watch. It spends the 6:45 payoff as a thumbnail that doesn't work. |

**Recommendation:** use A's thumbnail (fixed) with a title that names the hook: **"East Palestine: The Wheel Was on Fire. The Alarm Line Came From a Billing Rule."** A/B test it against A as written.

All three packages miss the question that brings this audience in, which is the vent and burn. The plan calls itself a two-number film, but every package sells only number one. Run a second test variant on number two, for example "East Palestine: The NTSB Says the Vent and Burn Wasn't Necessary. Here's the Number Behind It." It is the most-searched, most-argued part of the story, and it is the top comment on the most-watched film.

### Retention checkpoints

- **0:30 — mostly holds, with one leak.** 0:00–0:25 is excellent: a readout, a fire, a dark line that never lights. Then at 0:25: *"seeing it would not have prevented the derailment."* That is a fair fact in the wrong place. Thirty seconds in, it tells the viewer the first suspect is innocent before they have a suspect. Move it to 4:30 (Act II), where it belongs as part of explaining what an "alert" is.
- **The opening image is a reconstruction.** The real street-camera footage of the burning wheelset exists and has been widely shown. A channel whose brand is "the real document on screen" that opens on a reconstruction of real footage gets noticed. If it's a rights problem, solve it or say so on screen ("footage: [source]"). This is the single biggest credibility point in the first ten seconds.
- **2:30 — main swipe risk.** Act I ends on "five tank cars blown open three days later." Then the film says *"Act question: where did the margin run out?"* and drops into a 3D bearing. The viewer who came for "why did they blow it up" has been told it happened, has been promised nothing about it, and is now being shown a mechanism. The 0:55 open loop promises only the billing transcript. Add a second loop at 2:00–2:20, something like: "Three days later, a second number. We'll show where it came from, and where it didn't."
- **10:00 — likely losing them.** 8:30–10:30 is two minutes of 8-K, headcount and buybacks. Then 10:30 says the record doesn't link any of it. Then 11:30 goes back into the bearing. The engineers drift in the finance segment, and the finance people drift when the film disowns its own context. Expect the retention graph to sag at about 8:45 and again at about 11:30.

**Likely swipe points:** 0:30–0:45 (the concession, then the train-size beat), 2:25–2:45 (the mechanism with no promise), 9:00–10:00, 11:30 (déjà vu bearing), and 16:40. Reading the probable cause line by line plays as an ending, so viewers leave before "Who paid?", which is your most surprising data.

## 2. Viewers' real questions that are missing or answered late

**Answered too late:**
- **"Was it a lack of grease / a skipped repair?" (13:20).** This is the top-comment claim of the 1.29M-view film. Answering it at 13:20 means most of the people who believe it have already gone. Plant it at about 4:30 (when "the detector reads the outside" comes up) and pay it off at 13:20.
- **"Did blowing it up destroy the evidence?"** This is the other top comment, and the record lets you answer it without saying anything about motive. The evidence of *why the bearing failed* was erased by the bearing's own heat on night one, at car 23. The vent and burn three days later was on five *other* cars. If the claims file supports that sequence (C-ids around 13:20 suggest it does), say it plainly and put the time on screen. No other film can make this point.
- **"Why did they blow them up?" (14:50).** Much too late for the biggest question. See Q4.

**Missing:**
- **What polymerization is, and why a falling temperature matters.** Without a 20–30 s explainer, "138 → 126°F" and "153–158°F band" are meaningless to anyone who isn't a chemist. This is the hinge of signature 3.
- **Where did 153–158°F come from, and who gave it to the incident commander?** The plan says it was "not found in written guidance" but doesn't say who brought it into the room. Viewers will ask, and the answer matters for fairness.
- **Did train length matter?** The plan answers "how long" (0:40) but not "did 9,309 ft and 17,977 tons contribute?", which is what people are really asking. Give the NTSB's answer either way.
- **ECP brakes and the 2018 rule repeal, and crew size.** These fill the partisan comment sections. Cutting politicians is right. Cutting the factual question isn't. If the NTSB addressed whether different brakes or crew size would have changed the outcome, one attributed line on each disarms both camps. Check the claims file and use only what's there.
- **Why didn't firefighters know what was on board?** You cut placards and the consist delay, but it's a common question. One line, or a pinned-comment pointer.
- **Whose car was it, and who was responsible for inspecting it?** "Added at another railroad's yard" raises this, but the plan doesn't answer it.
- **Whose idea was the vent and burn, formally?** Who decided, who approved, and NS's position on the NTSB finding (see Q5).

## 3. The existing films versus this plan

**What they do better:**
- **Human stakes.** MPU and Bloomberg have residents, the black plume, and consequence. This plan cut health completely and never shows the plume. That's defensible editorially, but the plume is *the* image of the case. Show it as real footage at 2:00 and at 16:00 (when the charges fire at 4:37 p.m.). It is a record of the decision, not a health claim.
- **Human voices.** Real News has workers explaining their own jobs. This plan's only voices are text. The June 2023 NTSB investigative hearing was recorded. **Use the real audio of "Correct."** A typed word is a gotcha. A human voice with a real pause is evidence. If the docket has real detector or radio audio of the critical alarm, use that instead of typed text too.
- **Clear moral energy.** The advocacy films give the viewer someone to be angry at. You deliberately don't, so the documents have to provide the tension. That is why the pacing problems in Act III matter more here than they would in an advocacy film.
- **The railfan film is fluent in railway language.** Cowl Unit's audience will notice loose terms. Say "hot bearing detector" and "hotbox", get the milepost conventions right, and state units precisely.

**What only this plan does:**
- It is built from the final report and the docket.
- It shows that the alarm line comes from AAR Rule 36, a billing definition, in the industry's own words.
- It treats the second number, the 153–158°F band, as a threshold too. That gives two numbers and one mechanism, which is a genuinely original thesis.
- It shows the 26% reportable-defect finding, alongside the record's admission that it can't tie that to car 23.
- It reconciles NS's 10-K incident costs.
- It is honest about where the record stops. For a viewer who distrusts PR, that honesty is the main thing you're selling, so build it into the structure rather than one disclaimer.

## 4. Padding, repetition and cuts

| Beat | Problem | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 0:40 train size | A standalone fact card between two tense beats | Fold it into 0:00–0:25 as an on-screen caption, 5 s |
| 2:20 act-question card | Restates the 0:55 loop | Cut it, or replace it with the second open loop (vent and burn) |
| **2:30 vs 11:30** | **Two separate bearing-mechanism segments.** 11:30 rebuilds the bearing the viewer saw nine minutes earlier | **Merge.** Keep 2:30 for design, detection and heat lag. Make 11:30 a 40 s cascade that cuts directly to the failure |
| 12:30 ladder (signature 1) | The ladder appears at 0:00, 1:10, 5:10, 12:30 and 19:30. By 12:30 it adds nothing but the real-seconds count | Rebuild it (see Q6), or cut the 12:30 payoff down to the real-seconds count only |
| 8:30 + 9:30 | Two minutes on the 2019 plan | Cut to 60–70 s: one 8-K quote, one headcount figure, one buyback total, the incentive line. Stop listing buybacks by year |
| 14:00 DOT-111 | A detour into tank-car generations right before the film's biggest question | Move it to Act V, "Could it happen again?" (18:40), where it actually answers the question |

| 18:00 buybacks repeated | The second time the buybacks appear, now beside incident costs | Cut the juxtaposition (see Q5) |
| Signature 3's closing line | "Two numbers decided this town's week…" repeats the logline and sign-off option 2 | Fine once. Use it either here or as the sign-off, not both |

**Structural move:** the spine is two numbers, but number two gets about 2:30 of a 20:30 film, at 14:50. Restructure:

- Act I night one, 0:00–2:30
- Act II detection, 2:30–5:30
- Act III the first number, Correct., and a trimmed context section, 5:30–9:30
- Act IV the failure plus the grease and evidence answer, 9:30–11:30
- **Act V the second number, about 11:30–16:00** (three days compressed, polymerization explained, the falling temperature, the band, the meetings, Oxy not invited, the deadline, the plume, "not necessary", NS's response, the incident commander's words)
- Act VI record, costs and what's still legal, 16:00–20:00

## 5. Fairness and the split audience

**Norfolk Southern.**
- **Risky line (18:00):** *"NS 10-K: incident expenses… set beside the buybacks with 'no investigator has linked these'."* Putting two numbers side by side *is* the argument, and the disclaimer doesn't undo it. Finance viewers will call it having it both ways, and NS would be right to. Cut the buybacks from this beat.
- The cost figures also invite misreading. "Recoveries exceeding expenses by $254M (2025)" will be clipped as "NS made money on East Palestine." Show a **cumulative net figure** across 2023–2025 and say what the 2025 number is.
- At 16:00 the plan gives the NTSB finding that NS and its contractors created "unwarranted urgency", but it gives no NS response. In Act III you give the AAR and NS their full say. Do the same here. NS's on-record position on the vent-and-burn finding needs 10–15 s, attributed.
- "This car was never inspected by Norfolk Southern" is fair to NS and should stay prominent.

**The railroad industry (AAR).** "Correct." followed immediately by the AAR's other sentence is good practice. Also put **the Rule 36 text itself** on screen, and show any follow-up the witness gave in the same exchange. If the witness qualified the answer and you cut it, railroad viewers will find the transcript.

**The incident commander.** Mostly fair: his own account, his words about bearing responsibility, and Oxy not invited. To complete it, say what he was given and by whom. The fairness point is that he decided on the information in front of him, and the NTSB criticised how that information was framed. Make sure the 13-minute clock reads as pressure *on* him, not a failing *of* his.

**Regulators.**
- *"Advisories, not rules (to confirm)"* is still in the treatment. Resolve it before you script it. An unconfirmed claim about the FRA is exactly how you lose the engineers.
- Give the factual status of any federal detector-threshold rule.
- Give the NTSB recommendations with their status and date.

**Political split.** Cutting politicians and the bill fight is right, and this plan is cleaner than any of the four existing films. Three places could still lose one half:
- **The sign-off: *"The line that decides when a train stops is still the railroads' to set."*** That is a policy position (regulation should set it), voiced as the last word. Viewers who favour deregulation hear advocacy, and it contradicts "no side taken." Use a factual version: "Today, the line that decides when a train stops is set by each railroad. The NTSB has recommended [X]. As of [date], [status]."
- **"Accounting meets the laws of physics"** and "Wall St"-style framing. Keep editorial phrases like these out of the voiceover.
- **Leaving out the ECP brakes and crew-size questions entirely.** Each side reads that as protecting the other. One attributed NTSB line on each is the most even-handed move available.

## 6. Signature moments

**1. The ladder (12:30) — weak as written.** It is planted so often that the payoff is a repeat. Stronger version, **"The blind nineteen miles":** run the full 39 minutes from Salem to East Palestine, compressed to about 45 s, on a route line at true relative scale. Mileposts tick. There is no readout between detectors, because nobody could see. Optionally add an internal-temperature band clearly labelled ILLUSTRATIVE. The film's thesis is that the margin was spent in the gap, so show the gap. Then drop to **real time for the final 60 s**: critical alarm (real audio if it exists), brake air, 43 → 0 mph, 38 s. No music. Hold off showing the full ladder until this moment. At 0:00 and 1:10 show single readings only, not the ladder.

**2. "Correct." (6:45) — lands, if it's real audio.** Phrase-by-phrase highlighting of the question is good. The typed answer is the weakness. Real voice, room tone, a one-beat hold, then the AAR's counter-sentence. Put Rule 36 on screen right after.

**3. The second number (14:50) — strongest idea, worst-served.** It is late, crowded (falling temperature, band, 13:00 clock, deadline card, 16:37, Oxy), and comes after a DOT-111 detour.

- **Stronger version:** a single plot. One axis is time over the three days. One line is the hottest vinyl chloride car's temperature falling, 138 → 126°F. Above it sits a shaded band, 153–158°F, captioned "Source: not found in written guidance (NTSB)." The temperature line never approaches the band, and the viewer watches it fall away. Then a card: "Decision window: 13 minutes." Then "3:00 p.m. deadline." Then real footage of the plume at 4:37 p.m. Then the NTSB: "not necessary."
- **Fix the time formats.** The plan mixes "20:13 EST", "8:52", "13:00 decision clock" (13 minutes or 1 p.m.?), "4:37 p.m." and "16:37". For a US/UK audience, use 12-hour clock with the date on every card, and write the clock as "13 MINUTES".

**The single best idea for making this the definitive film: "The comments against the record."** Take the beliefs that fill every other film's comments and test each one on screen against the docket, with document IDs:
- "A $7 tube of grease"
- "They blew it up because it was cheapest"
- "It destroyed the evidence"
- "Cost cutting caused it"
- "The brake rule repeal"

Each claim gets a verdict in the film's own clinical voice: *Supported / Not supported / The record can't say*. Make them YouTube chapters ("Was it the grease?", "Did the burn destroy the evidence?"). Publish a sourced timeline with docket numbers in the description. No other film engages the folk theories with documents. It turns the "no side taken" stance from a constraint into the product, and it gives search a reason to rank this film for the questions people actually type.

## The 5 most important changes, ranked

1. **Make number two a full act and promise it early.** Add a second open loop by 2:20. Move the vent and burn to about 11:30, give it about 4:30, explain polymerization, build signature 3 as one clean plot, show real plume footage, and add NS's response. Test a package on it.
2. **Use the real record, not reconstructions.** Real street-camera footage at 0:00, real hearing audio for "Correct.", Rule 36 text on screen, and real alarm audio if the docket has it. Fix "103° above ambient" everywhere, and standardise time formats.
3. **Cut the repeats.** Merge the two bearing segments, cut the finance section to about 60 s, merge Act V's "what changed" and "again", move DOT-111 to the end, and rebuild signature 1 as "the blind nineteen miles" ending in 60 s of real time.
4. **Answer the comment-section claims against the record, early and on screen.** Grease, "destroyed the evidence" (bearing evidence lost night one, the burn was on other cars, three days later), cost cutting, ECP brakes, crew size, train length. Each gets a clinical verdict, chapters and a sourced description.
5. **Close the fairness gaps.** Remove the buybacks-beside-costs juxtaposition at 18:00. Show cumulative net incident cost. Resolve "(to confirm)" on the FRA. Replace the sign-off line *"still the railroads' to set"* with a factual status line on the NTSB recommendation. Strip editorial phrasing ("Accounting meets the laws of physics") from the voiceover.
